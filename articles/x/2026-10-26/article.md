Tesco is [moving its store computing off VMware and onto open-source Proxmox](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/10/26/tesco-replacing-vmware-with-proxmox-in-its-stores-and-on-the-edge/5305281), the same retailer that says VMware once offered it a 175 percent price increase. Meanwhile LiveKit says its turn detector [cuts voice-agent callers off 4.5 percent of the time, against 9.9 percent for the best alternative](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=795s).

Here is what happened in AI and DevOps this cycle.

UK retail giant Tesco is hiring a Head of Software Engineering for its edge platform whose job is to [own "Tesco's migration from VMware to Proxmox across the edge estate"](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/10/26/tesco-replacing-vmware-with-proxmox-in-its-stores-and-on-the-edge/5305281) and build a software-driven platform to provision, monitor and maintain the servers in its stores. The ad warns that store tech must keep working through connectivity drops and constrained local resources across thousands of locations, and that a botched change hits store operations directly. Tesco runs more than 5,000 stores worldwide and is already suing VMware in a licensing dispute in which it says VMware offered a 175 percent price increase; hearings are likely in 2027, and The Register hears core workloads may go to Hyper-V. Proxmox only added 24/7 enterprise support in September, so when a retailer this size bets its stores on it, the "VMware alternative" question has moved from homelabs to the checkout lane.

The "Claude got nerfed" rumors may be mostly about phrasing. Andrew Curran points out that [reverse engineering and stripping malware from your own devices sits so close to the cyber guardrails](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2114537575620980868) that word choice decides between a refusal and smooth sailing, and commenters in the Reddit threads behind the rumor say Claude still does it if you rephrase. He relays that OpenAI calls similar Codex refusals accidental decision-boundary problems that are being fixed, with the reply he quotes saying there is no prohibition on reverse engineering with Codex, and he expects Anthropic to do the same. His argument: blocking this only pushes people to less restricted models and costs subscriptions.

SallyAnn DeLucia, head of product at Arize AI, ran a workshop on [how to write your first AI eval](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0), and it opens with the "vibes problem": you tweak a prompt Monday, it feels better, and [a week later the use case you fixed is broken](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=126s) with no way to prove what changed. Without evals you [can't catch regressions, compare model versions or put quality gates in CI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=158s). Her framing: [traces are the logs for AI and evals are the tests](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=236s), so you need observability before you can judge anything.

Her recipe for the judge itself is refreshingly concrete. Swap vague words like "helpful" and "accurate" for [checks such as "answers the user's follow-up first" and "keeps totals within the stated budget"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=1160s), and [judge one quality per eval](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=1192s), because she says research shows an LLM judge handles one criterion at a time best. Then [start small: 20 traces, one dimension, one judge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=2066s), read the explanations and grow from there. As she puts it, [a metric tells you something might be broken, but an eval tells you the failure mode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gv4w23xYj-0&t=2038s).

Jesse Hall of LiveKit, the framework he says [powers ChatGPT's advanced voice mode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=212s), explained [how to build voice agents that don't fall apart when you interrupt them](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo). His first warning is about dashboards: a text-to-speech dashboard can show [210 milliseconds to first byte while popular providers ship up to three quarters of a second of silence after it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=425s), so measure time to first audio, and for the LLM [time to first sentence rather than first token](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=488s). For slow back-end lookups, he says [one line that makes the tool call async](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=579s) hands the mic back so the agent keeps talking, the way a human says "give me a second, I'm looking that up."

The rest of the talk is about the window between the caller's last syllable and the agent's first sound. Hall says the call [holds at about one and a half seconds and starts to feel human at 500 to 600 milliseconds](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=881s). LiveKit's turn detector listens to rhythm, pitch and intonation as well as the transcript, so someone saying "five hundred fifty-five twenty-three" doesn't get cut off at "five hundred"; on LiveKit's own hotel test it [cut users off 4.5 percent of the time against 9.9 percent for the best alternative, with the eval harness open source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=795s). Picking models off a leaderboard misses all of this, so his team runs simulated callers who interrupt, mumble and change their minds, then swaps models. On their hotel agent, time to first sentence was [381 milliseconds for Gemma 4 31B on LiveKit's inference, 844 for the same model on OpenRouter and 1,645 for GPT-5.5](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OmyqelTwpOo&t=1125s), the kind of gap no leaderboard shows.

ICANN's chief technology officer John Crain says [the DNS probably has less than ten years to become quantum safe](https://www.theregister.com/networks/2026/10/26/work-to-protect-dns-from-quantum-decryption-attacks-is-speeding-up/5305274): "I don't know if it's three years or five, but it's probably not ten." The catch is size. Bigger DNSSEC keys mean bigger responses that may need TCP instead of the single UDP packets DNS prefers, and he says preparing to go from UDP to TCP will take a few years. Anyone running resolvers or firewalls that quietly drop DNS over TCP has a long-dated homework assignment.

China added 23 technologies tied to large models, agents and computing clusters to its export-restricted list in mid-October, then [let two companies, named elsewhere as DeepSeek and MiniMax, keep exporting](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/26/after-threatening-ai-export-bans-china-allows-deepseek-to-keep-selling/5305267). Open-source releases are exempt, which matters for anyone running open weights. The catalog also lets Beijing stop exports of AI it sees as sovereign capacity, such as potent bug-finders.

The AI datacenter boom wobbled in Australia. Datacenter operator Firmus [called off an IPO that aimed to raise about 5 billion dollars at a valuation above 40 billion](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/26/after-threatening-ai-export-bans-china-allows-deepseek-to-keep-selling/5305267) after investors balked at its revenue forecasts, and NEXTDC's shares fell almost 40 percent after its power supplier halted work on an expansion amid local protests. Power, not GPUs, keeps turning out to be the constraint.

Lauren Tan is [excited about the coming wave of new programming languages](https://x.com/poteto/status/2114608724312752550), quoting Matt Pocock's wish list for the next language built for agents: as fast as Rust, as little design space as Go, and the strictest type and verification system ever written, with Bend as an interesting first try. If agents write most of the code, a language tuned for the checker rather than the typist starts to make sense.
