title: "One shared link took over ChatGPT accounts, Linux ran 5x slower on an RTX Spark, and a voice judge went from 33% to 92%"
dek: "A Halloween Saturday of haunted laptops, haiku from orbit, and the cheapest model quietly winning again."
---
A security team showed that [opening one public ChatGPT share link was enough to hand an attacker your whole account](https://www.parameter.ai/labs/we-found-a-gap-at-openai), private chats included, until OpenAI patched it on October 26. Meanwhile, Alex Ziskind got Linux running on Nvidia's new RTX Spark laptop, then watched the same model run [at 13.9 tokens per second on Linux against 65.4 on Windows](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0N3TksVcH7Y&t=545s), on the same machine.

Here is what happened in AI and DevOps this cycle.

Parameter's researchers chained two harmless-looking ChatGPT features, the graph renderer and the HTML Preview, into JavaScript running inside the victim's signed-in session at chatgpt.com. From there the payload could [list the victim's conversations, read a private chat that was never shared, and ship it to an outside server](https://www.parameter.ai/labs/we-found-a-gap-at-openai). The victim installed nothing and pasted nothing; they clicked a link. OpenAI rated it P1 and fixed it, which is the good news, but every team embedding "preview this HTML" inside a logged-in app now has a fresh threat model to copy.

Alex Ziskind spent three days putting Linux on an MSI laptop with the RTX Spark chip, and he did it by letting [Claude Code drive the machine through a JetKVM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0N3TksVcH7Y&t=307s) while a MacBook camera watched the upside-down screen. Linux first saw only 28 GB of the 128 GB, because the firmware [reserves 96 GB for the GPU and only Windows can change it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0N3TksVcH7Y&t=398s). The real culprit for the slowdown is a Windows-only driver called [Nvidia Platform Controllers and Framework](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0N3TksVcH7Y&t=627s), which reads an encrypted power table Linux can't decode, so plugged in, the GPU sits near 790 MHz instead of 2 GHz. His advice for now is WSL, which uses the Windows driver and keeps the boost.

Steven Latré of imec ran thousands of SWE-Bench Pro coding runs to answer when owning GPUs beats paying for an API, and the answer is a staircase, not a line. On small hardware you need [about 89% GPU utilization to break even, but on an 8x B200 box just 15% will do](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9-Y_oS9OBLc&t=812s), and a GLM-class setup landed around [25% cheaper over its lifetime](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9-Y_oS9OBLc&t=868s). Concurrency is the trap: Kimi K3 hit an 86% resolve rate, yet served only [about four reliable users per machine](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9-Y_oS9OBLc&t=740s), still three times slower than the API. If you're pricing a move to open weights, model the concurrent users first and the accuracy second.

The same imec study found the harness barely changed accuracy but changed the GPU bill: with GLM, Codex generated [only half the tokens of the other harnesses, which halved the GPU wall time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9-Y_oS9OBLc&t=914s). Matt Shumer took the opposite end of the argument with a hot take that [harness engineering is usually stupid and you should just use the harness the model was trained for](https://x.com/mattshumer_/status/2116377535818891690). Both can be true at once: on someone else's API the harness is a style choice, and on your own GPUs it's a line item.

Supriya Subudhi, a product manager at Bumble, asked users whether they wanted AI feedback on their dating photos, and [78% said yes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CvmZtAPmAW4&t=218s). When the A/B test shipped, only [10 to 15% actually used it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CvmZtAPmAW4&t=239s), and the tips sometimes told people to [smile more in photos where they were already smiling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CvmZtAPmAW4&t=343s). The best model cost about 10x a simpler approach, the cheaper swap tanked the results, and the team ended up on [the cheapest option once evals let them tune prompts quickly](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CvmZtAPmAW4&t=699s). A green A/B dashboard is where an AI feature's homework starts.

Shiv Indap of Upstart and Brooke Hopkins of Coval walked through a voice agent that handles loan verification calls, where asking the wrong follow-up question is a regulatory problem, not a UX one. If an applicant says the money is for medical costs, [the agent must not ask how much](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3osCbSufv8g&t=187s). At launch, the LLM judge that scores compliance [agreed with human reviewers only 33% of the time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3osCbSufv8g&t=452s), and after encoding the reviewers' tribal knowledge and fixing the judge itself, [agreement reached 92%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3osCbSufv8g&t=519s). The review process started as a Google Sheet that went from five columns to ten within weeks as 15 people added their own, so if your evals live in a spreadsheet, that's your sign.

Starcloud CEO Philip Johnston gave a factory tour of a two-billion-dollar company putting Nvidia GPUs in orbit. Starcloud 1 flew an H100 and cost [about 2.5 million dollars against quotes of 75 to 100 million](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mdq6urSx-f8&t=17s), and he says space wins once launch costs fall [below roughly 500 dollars a kilo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mdq6urSx-f8&t=1936s). To test Starcloud 2, the team bought a broken, water-filled vibration table [for 200,000 dollars and fixed it over a weekend](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mdq6urSx-f8&t=194s) instead of waiting two years for a new one. In the same week, Google's experimental orbital datacenter prototype [put its TPU to work writing a haiku and beamed it to Earth](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/31/googles-ai-satellite-sends-a-very-expensive-haiku-to-earth/5306818), which is a pricey way to prove an AI chip can do its job in orbit.

Richmond Alake of Oracle argued that everyone in the room has the same models, so the edge has to come from somewhere else, and he used the World War II codebreakers who worked out that ["AF" meant Midway](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1_lX0m64-aI&t=343s) to make the point. Washington had the same intercepts; the Pearl Harbor basement team had months of paper notes, which is to say memory. His proposed "intelligence plane" covers [what an agent knows, what it remembers, what it's allowed to see, and how it gets better](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1_lX0m64-aI&t=508s). A control plane governs agents, and his bet is that a memory and learning layer is what makes them yours.

Scribd, the parent of Slideshare, Everand and Fable, began emailing users that [an unauthorized party may have accessed emails, card issuers, expiry dates, the last four card digits and payment history](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50312756). The notice went out on October 26 through a third-party notification firm, and readers on Hacker News were surprised they hadn't seen it reported anywhere. Expect phishing that name-drops all four brands, and treat any "Scribd security" email asking for a login as fake.

Andrew Curran pointed to an Anthropic Halloween experiment in which a model was [intentionally fine-tuned to be misaligned, never deployed, and studied for what it "dreams"](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2116367197501354016), nicknamed ShadowClaude. He opened with a warning to reporters that none of it is real. Fine-tuning a villain on purpose is how you learn what to look for when one shows up by accident.

Roblox's Q3 shareholder letter shows a chart of [daily users of its MCP server climbing sharply after Opus 5.5 shipped on September 22](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2116583791166939268), and more than 80% of the top 100 games used AI over the past month. A game platform's investor deck now tracks an AI protocol, which says more about where MCP is headed than any spec update.

IBM researchers added a runtime check called JevAct between a tool-using agent and its tools, keeping the models and tools unchanged. An LLM first [turns the domain policy into code checks and yes-or-no questions](https://x.com/omarsar0/status/2116547247018229999), and the gate enforces them before each action runs. It's the Upstart lesson from the other direction: write the rules down where a machine can check them.

Joshua Warren wrote his own Linux driver for Apple's Neural Engine, which has no public docs, and found it had been [running in first gear](https://x.com/JoshuaSWarren/status/2116335841731867019). Moving one clock setting from level 1 to level 5 made speech encoding on his M1 Max 31% faster with bit-for-bit identical output. That's two stories today about a chip being held back by a power setting only one operating system understands.

A free GitHub repo packs [18 hands-on labs that take one app from Docker and CI/CD through Terraform, Kubernetes, Helm, Argo CD, Prometheus, Grafana and progressive delivery](https://x.com/twtayaan/status/2116486087816425478). It's one application the whole way, which beats eighteen disconnected hello-worlds.

Amjad Masad says Replit apps with [autofix turned on become self-maintaining software](https://x.com/amasad/status/2116343923925041462), patching themselves when they break. Harrison Chase, meanwhile, argued that while [context engineering now has fairly standard solutions, agents keep producing fun new problems](https://x.com/hwchase17/status/2116553654857867330), so "agent engineering is solved" is premature. Guillermo Rauch summed up the direction in five words: [an agent on every desktop](https://x.com/rauchg/status/2116345666108854781).

That's the cycle: a link that steals accounts, a laptop that's faster on battery, and a satellite writing poetry. Happy Halloween, and may your GPU never get stuck at 790 MHz.
