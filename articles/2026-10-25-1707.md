A mathematician who studies fluid equations says an OpenAI paper is [unbelievable on a technical level, "maybe beyond what a human could have come up with but not beyond human understanding"](https://x.com/gdb/status/2114483026608755082). Meanwhile Claude Code sessions in different terminals can now [pass findings to each other without you copy-pasting](https://x.com/mirku21/status/2114462105512644974).

Here is what happened in AI and DevOps this cycle.

Greg Brockman shared [Scott Armstrong's verdict on OpenAI's fluid blow-up construction](https://x.com/gdb/status/2114483026608755082). Armstrong, a math professor at Courant/NYU and research director at CNRS, says the trick has more to do with convex integration than anything else: instead of building oscillations by hand, the paper lets a fluid instability generate them, drawing energy from the background shear until the stress becomes the force that creates a collapsing smooth vortex. He says using a physical instability that way has no precedent, that several groups of PDE experts now more or less understand it, and that anyone calling the result unsurprising is wrong. When working mathematicians say a machine-assisted construction is nowhere in the literature, the "AI can't do original math" argument gets a lot harder to hold.

Claude Code's cross-session messaging lets separate sessions [discover each other and send plain-text messages over local sockets](https://x.com/mirku21/status/2114462105512644974), switched on with a crossSessionInbound setting, and the [official docs](http://code.claude.com/docs/en/cross-session-messaging) say Claude finds the target with ListAgents and sends with SendMessage, so you never call either tool yourself. The pitch is to put Opus 5.5 on architecture and sign-off, Sonnet 5.5 on code and Haiku 5.5 on relaying, so no window burns context on another's history. A sibling setup [runs Sonnet as the main session with Opus as an on-call advisor](https://x.com/xsmryze/status/2114477369276944515) and three read-only Haiku subagents for search, grep and diff audits, the audit one isolated in a throwaway worktree, while a third [goes further with an Opus lead and three Haiku teammates that self-claim tasks](https://x.com/Grirku/status/2114457089007374740). These come from hype accounts, and their speed figures and a routing layer called "JEV" aren't in the docs, but the core features are real. The practical idea is simple: let the expensive model think only at the moments that need thinking.

Hassan El Mghari laid out [how he builds apps these days](https://x.com/nutlope/status/2114445139800461618) in six steps. He records a 1-5 minute spoken prompt, adds screenshots so the agent sees what he has in mind, uses Matt Pocock's grill-me skill so the agent asks clarifying questions first, then sends feedback as another voice note. The detail worth stealing is step five: for each new feature he asks the agent for a quick screen recording proving it works before merging the PR. He runs Claude Code with Opus 5.5 alongside OpenCode with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3, and gives the agents Vercel and GitHub CLIs so they can push and deploy on their own.

Lauren Tan, an engineer at SpaceXAI, shared a [skill that teaches coding agents to verify their own work](https://x.com/0xChaseTM/status/2114487798648467787). It turns every check into a record of three parts: the exact launch command and readiness signal, reproducible user actions with fixed inputs, and an observable result with evidence like output, a file or a trace. Every check ends as PASS, FAIL or NOT_RUN, and when a failure repeats a /correct step encodes the lesson as the strongest constraint available, from a type to a lint rule to a regression test. In [a separate hour-long talk](https://x.com/shirosignal/status/2114446693622239672) she describes running a team of more than 35 Grok Bot agents: a chief-of-staff agent, three managers and 33 workers. The common thread is that the next agent should hit a failing check before a reviewer ever sees the same mistake.

A developer open-sourced [Octopus OS](https://x.com/RoundtableSpace/status/2114458224816505065), an agent harness where agents can edit their own harness in the middle of a task. It took 8 months and sits on a 45,000-line Rust kernel, keeps all system state in JSON files the agents can rewrite, and its desktop app is built on the Codex source. If self-modifying harnesses hold up outside a demo, "improve the agent" stops being a separate step and becomes part of the job.

Dassie Spivack, who used to be the person companies called after a hack, argues that [the cover-up sinks companies, not the breach](https://x.com/mattshumer_/status/2114448048168357950). The receipts: Uber paid hackers 100,000 dollars to stay quiet in 2016 and later settled for 148 million with all 50 states, Yahoo's 500-million-account breach cut 350 million off Verizon's price, and Equifax waited 40 days to disclose while an executive sold stock and went to prison. The account is new and promoting its own framework, but the advice is cheap: tell customers early, even if all you can say is "we're investigating."

A quick one. A [curated list of prompt and context engineering papers, tools and courses](https://x.com/GithubProjects/status/2114443382563217730) comes with a beginner learning path, handy if your agent setups keep growing faster than your understanding of why they work.

That's the cycle. Give your expensive model fewer chores, make your agents prove it works, and see you tomorrow.
