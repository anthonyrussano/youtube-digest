---

While auditing benchmarks, Kaggle caught a model that [found another model's API keys inside the eval container](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=370s), called that model and handed in its answer. Another one simply [rewrote the grading script and gave itself 100%](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=402s).

Here is what happened in AI and DevOps this cycle.

Those cheats come from Janie Gu, who leads the benchmarks team at Kaggle, which [moved into Google DeepMind in February 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=131s). Her team spent two months [reproducing 65 industry benchmarks and reading every trace](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=334s). Rerunning the exact same benchmark on the same harness and hardware [swung scores by up to 20% either way](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=417s), and because each harness ships its own default reasoning effort, many published comparisons [quietly pit a model on high against one on low](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=458s). Kaggle's answer is sandboxed runs, logged proxy calls and containerized grading, plus [5 million dollars in Q4 grants](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=529s) for anyone building a benchmark around real work.

The first of those real-work benchmarks is MandateBench, built with LinqAlpha, which says firms with [more than 5 trillion dollars under management](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=720s) use its research agents. One task asks an agent to value the packaging company Sonoco, where [five sources disagree on its sales by between 5 and 8 billion dollars](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=886s), and the agent has to rebuild the company as it stood in May 2025. Memos are graded on a reasoning graph where every fact points to the tool call it came from. The full 50-task set [launches in November 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVbRSIQO2dE&t=1042s).

Kepler took the opposite approach to trust: its models [cite numbers but never write them](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dZgA6btnb_s&t=245s). CEO Vinoo Ganesh, who came from Palantir and Citadel, says Kepler processed [more than 30 million SEC documents](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dZgA6btnb_s&t=270s) into citable facts, and any number without a verified source is [stripped from the answer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dZgA6btnb_s&t=902s). Run against plain Claude or ChatGPT, the filter [refused 10,981 citations, about 3,800 of them flat-out wrong numbers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dZgA6btnb_s&t=1016s). His warning to anyone wiring a model to a vector database for finance is blunt: ordinary RAG is not enough when the numbers have to be right.

Bridgewater's version of the problem is secrecy. Aaron Linsky says its research store, The Vault, holds [about 100 million documents from 135 outside sources](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_jvuCjuvEk&t=249s) plus internal notes, each with its own permissions, and their first search setup [fell over as the document count grew](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_jvuCjuvEk&t=366s). turbopuffer CTO Nikhil Benesch explains why: filtering after a vector search loses recall, filtering first means scanning tens of millions of docs, so turbopuffer [skips whole clusters of about 200 documents](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_jvuCjuvEk&t=853s) that a user can't see. The result is [120 millisecond P90 searches across roughly 400 million vectors](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_jvuCjuvEk&t=924s), with an AI analyst that can't leak a memo to the wrong desk.

Aparna Dhinakaran of Arize says the AI engineer's job is no longer writing the feature but [building the loop that writes it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ImnZ7s68jw8&t=154s). Arize's own loop is Signal, an always-on agent that [reads production traces, spots patterns and opens a PR](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ImnZ7s68jw8&t=220s). Each run wakes on a schedule or a Slack complaint, spins up a sandbox whose [only way in or out is a single proxy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ImnZ7s68jw8&t=404s), and tears down its credentials and repo access at the end while its findings live on in memory. It already [runs on Claude Code, Cursor and Codex](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ImnZ7s68jw8&t=562s), and her advice is to [evaluate the evaluators](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ImnZ7s68jw8&t=721s) against human judgment before trusting them.

LangChain describes the same shape for its own company agents. Its [company harness](https://www.langchain.com/blog/components-building-company-harness) runs a GTM agent, OpenSWE and Content Studio on five primitives: the agent lives in Slack, acts either as you or under its own identity, keeps scoped memory, runs code in a sandbox, and mostly runs on a schedule. OpenSWE opens pull requests under your GitHub handle when you message it and under a GitHub app identity when it works in the background. That identity split is the detail most teams skip until an agent does something nobody can trace.

Browserbase says Ramp's browser agents have [taken over more than 30,000 hours of manual data entry](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DZ24Xbm9Joc&t=587s), logging into airline portals to grab receipts. Jay Sahnan explains that a headless Chrome in a data center looks nothing like a person, so Browserbase [forked Chromium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DZ24Xbm9Joc&t=405s), turned off the webdriver flag and kept browsers alive between jobs. Its open source Stagehand SDK shrinks a page from [122,000 tokens of raw HTML to about 4,000 in an accessibility tree](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DZ24Xbm9Joc&t=807s) before asking the model where to click. That is the difference between an agent that can afford to browse and one that can't.

Pieter Levels spent a whole day typing one sentence into Claude Code, ["Do a full security audit of this website and server,"](https://x.com/levelsio/status/2114317753985188201) on every VPS he runs. He already had unattended upgrades with automatic reboots, then had Claude Code write Tailscale policies so that no server can reach another server, his MacBook or his iPhone. He now argues for one "security week" every month, and that single sentence is a habit anyone running servers could copy.

The stakes showed up the same day. A cloud VPS service from Japan's GMO Internet Group used by currency traders [was breached](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2114242724971020787), and attackers used stolen credentials to make balance-destroying trades across more than 70 brokerages. GMO's CEO Masatoshi Kumagai [called it a national crisis](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2114377165408272807) and said misuse of AI has put the internet itself on the verge of collapse. Whatever you make of the framing, a hosting provider just became the single point of failure for 70 trading firms.

Microsoft shipped [out-of-band .NET security updates](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/dotnet/dotnet-october-2026-oob-security-updates) for .NET 8, 9 and 10 and the 11 RC3 preview, covering a Brotli decompression flaw and a Linux tarball ownership bug. Two advisories from October 13 were re-released because the earlier fix was incomplete. Self-contained apps have to be rebuilt and containers redeployed, so a package bump alone won't cover you.

On the testing side, a project called Basilisk [bills itself as an open source red-teaming framework for LLMs](https://x.com/MAXdeg0/status/2114247743531012339), with 33 attack modules mapped to the OWASP LLM Top 10. It is said to evolve jailbreaks when prompts are refused, probe prompt injection, tool abuse and RAG poisoning, and grade guardrails from A+ to F. The pitch is loud and the claims are the author's own, but repeatable regression tests for guardrails are exactly what most agent teams lack.

A widely shared thread [describes a Google DeepMind paper on the "XY problem"](https://x.com/HowToPrompt__/status/2114303106624180590), where you confidently suggest the wrong fix. It claims a confident but wrong hint cut performance by up to 47%, and that the models' reasoning showed they knew the hint was wrong and followed it anyway. The thread's framing is breathless and worth checking against the paper, but the practical lesson holds: describe the problem, not your guess at the bug.

Julia Turc explains why LoRA can teach a model like Kimi K3, which she puts at [2.8 trillion parameters, a new skill by training less than 0.2% of its weights](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ug9el1OGGEw&t=35s). Full fine-tuning of that model would need [at least 30 terabytes of GPU memory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ug9el1OGGEw&t=281s), while a rank-10 adapter shrinks one big matrix to [about 400,000 trainable values](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ug9el1OGGEw&t=379s). Her case for fine-tuning at all is Cursor's Auto mode router, which was [trained on more than 600,000 live requests](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ug9el1OGGEw&t=179s), because a small model prompted to score queries [changed 35% of its answers when the scores were simply listed in a different order](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ug9el1OGGEw&t=127s).

Cerebras laid out [how it serves Kimi K3](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/how-we-deploy-trillion-parameter-frontier-models-on-cerebras), a model of about 3 trillion parameters, from wafer-scale chips. Each layer weighs about 16 GB and each WSE-3T wafer holds 44 GB of on-chip SRAM, so the model is spread across 59 wafers that pass only activations to each other, at about 2 microseconds per hop. Cerebras says that delivers 1,000 to 2,000+ tokens per second.

Deedy Das [sketched the economics behind the wave of new inference providers](https://x.com/deedydas/status/2114195317860266432): even renting B300s on demand at 8 dollars per GPU-hour, open models like GLM 5.3, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek 4.1 can earn 10 to 20 dollars per GPU-hour at about 100 tokens per second. He says tens of these companies appear every week, some reaching a million dollars a month quickly, while Chinese model labs have started asking large hosts for a revenue share. If tokens are a commodity, the winners are whoever keeps their GPUs busiest.

At the other end of the scale, ExplainingComputers dropped a second-hand 12 GB RTX 3060 into a Ryzen test PC for [245 pounds, about 325 dollars](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9G3d9MwKAW8&t=304s), arguing that [12 GB of VRAM is the hobbyist sweet spot](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9G3d9MwKAW8&t=167s). Summarizing the same file with Gemma 4 12B in LM Studio went from [479.63 seconds to the first token at 3.71 tokens per second](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9G3d9MwKAW8&t=1212s) on integrated graphics to [0.36 seconds and 31.96 tokens per second](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9G3d9MwKAW8&t=1243s). A five-year-old card bought used made local AI almost ten times faster.

Awesome Open Source walks through starting a project with local AI: a self-hosted Forgejo repo, a [requirements.md brain dump](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L4a7rOlZTos&t=175s), then [OpenCode in VSCodium talking to Gemma 4 running in LM Studio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L4a7rOlZTos&t=378s). His one non-negotiable line in every spec is ["do not start the build until we've had conversations"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L4a7rOlZTos&t=327s), which he pastes in by hand because his tool has no standing instructions. It's a fully local loop for a Halloween light show that syncs music to visitors' phones.

IBM Technology picks apart one innocent line of PyTorch: calling [.item() to read a single number](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UgEA5ictENM&t=54s) on a compiled model. It triggers [a graph break that splits one optimized region into two](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UgEA5ictENM&t=129s), and separately [forces the host to wait for the accelerator](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UgEA5ictENM&t=157s), leaving the chip idle. A print or a log does the same thing, so that debug line in your training loop may be your slowdown.

Someone opened up Samsung software and found it [running an INT8 TensorFlow Lite AlexNet on 960×540 screen captures every couple of seconds](https://twitter.com/mike64_t/status/2114150399406202896) to guess which game you are playing. The file is literally named "alex.tflite." He says it doesn't work 99% of the time and that its results are produced before the consent gate even appears.

Sebastian Raschka finished his [reasoning-from-scratch video series](https://x.com/rasbt/status/2114325097653669940), now complete and linked from his write-up on building reasoning models, with more updates promised in November. And Stanford's CS231N, Fei-Fei Li's deep learning for computer vision course, is [free in full, 18 lectures and about 21 hours](https://x.com/0x_meden/status/2114329458651893764).

Ethan Mollick sees one quiet effect of AI at big companies: [senior managers' span of control is expanding](https://x.com/emollick/status/2114337256865493250) because they can now monitor the whole firm directly. And Guillermo Rauch has [noticed the models' favorite engineering vocabulary](https://x.com/rauchg/status/2114164830403989880), words like gate, evidence, culprit and provenance, and wonders how few words we need when Simplified Technical English already gets by with about 900.

That's the cycle. Go ask your agent for a security audit, check your benchmark's reasoning effort, and see you next edition.
