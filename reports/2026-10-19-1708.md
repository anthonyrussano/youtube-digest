A 27B model built to improve its own research process [proposed hypotheses that matched papers published after its training cutoff 6.3% of the time](https://x.com/omarsar0/status/2112295238664696237), against 2.5% for GPT-6. And Coinbase's rule for letting an agent work alone fits in one line: [the loop is only as trustworthy as the check](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI&t=652s).

Here is what happened in AI and DevOps this cycle.

Evan Kormos, a player coach at Coinbase with 15 years in enterprise financial services, laid out [how to know when an AI agent can work unsupervised](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI). Every agent starts [supervised on day one](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI&t=877s), with experts checking all of its output. Once measured confidence reaches a policy threshold, [humans only sample a slice of the work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI&t=907s) while traces cover the rest, and finally [people handle only the exceptions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI&t=931s). His recipe for regulated workflows like lending is to [keep it deterministic on the outside and adaptive on the inside](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI&t=965s), and he picks agent-run steps by asking whether each one [can be checked against policy, run in a bounded loop, be traced and fall back to a human](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_4Ph2dhmUFI&t=819s).

Amazon Bedrock now supports [structured outputs for every Anthropic Claude model](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/structured-outputs-for-anthropic-claude-models-on-amazon-bedrock/), using constrained decoding so the model simply cannot produce tokens that break your JSON schema. The first request with a new schema compiles a grammar, which is then cached for 24 hours, and renaming fields or editing descriptions keeps that cache warm. A strict tool-use mode also locks agent tool arguments to their schema, which AWS says removes the retries and parsing code that malformed JSON used to need.

AWS showed an [AI agent in AWS Transform that builds the business case for moving self-managed Kafka to Amazon MSK](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQX30d3ETB8), a job AWS says [takes weeks by hand and now takes minutes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQX30d3ETB8&t=41s). It sizes [Express brokers from your actual throughput, not your current broker count](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQX30d3ETB8&t=79s), and in the demo it [flagged that the uploaded inventory was missing throughput data](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQX30d3ETB8&t=258s) and took the numbers in chat instead. It produces [a workbook, a report and a presentation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQX30d3ETB8&t=346s), and it can be driven [from your own AI assistant through an MCP server](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQX30d3ETB8&t=54s).

The paper behind the cold open is DiscoveryRSI, which applies recursive self-improvement to scientific hypotheses in four life-science domains, as [Elvis (@omarsar0) summarized it](https://x.com/omarsar0/status/2112295238664696237). It grows trees of hypotheses with a multi-agent evolutionary search, credits the ones that led somewhere, and replays its own research runs to spot repeated failures. After two rounds, automated hypothesis quality rose from 4.86 to 5.39, with the biggest gains in falsifiability, a hint that the scientific method itself can become a training signal.

OpenAI's developer team cooked ramen on camera while showing off [a refreshed Codex CLI that can fork a conversation into a separate worktree](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQHtmW9ZlIs&t=81s) and lets you talk to your terminal. They also demoed [Codex Cloud, which runs your agent in a team-customized cloud environment so you can close your laptop](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQHtmW9ZlIs&t=50s), and a sous chef running on [GPT Live One, a full-duplex voice model that doesn't interrupt when you trail off](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQHtmW9ZlIs&t=148s). One host's favorite community build used a tool called Astra to [decode a Napoleon letter that had gone unsolved for two or three hundred years](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQHtmW9ZlIs&t=361s).

One Claude Code power user shared a [three-model setup where Sonnet 5.5 writes the code, Haiku 5.5 subagents search the repo in parallel, and Opus 5.5 only advises](https://x.com/thedelost/status/2112294434780131625). In that setup, Opus steps in at three moments: before a plan locks, when the same error shows up twice, and before the work is called done. The post also plugs an unrelated routing product, so treat it as one builder's recipe, but keeping your most expensive model as a reviewer instead of the main worker is a cheap idea to try.

Hao AI Lab released [FastVideo FastH3-QAD-v2 and new NVFP4 video decoders](https://x.com/haoailab/status/2112305087225983427), and generated an entire 768p clip with audio in 8 steps on just two NVIDIA DGX Spark desktops. Video generation with sound is moving from data-center racks to boxes that fit on a desk.

AWS also cut the sign-up wall for its ML studio: new customers can now [land in a ready SageMaker Studio environment in about 20 seconds](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/developers-can-now-access-sagemaker-studio-through-the-new-aws-experience/) after signing in with Google, GitHub, Apple or Amazon, and most need no credit card. They get 100 dollars in Free Tier credits, with a chance at 100 more, and usage stops on its own when the credits run out, so there's no surprise bill.

The same team says [SageMaker JumpStart added more than 100 models from 30 providers](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/amazon-sagemaker-jumpstart-2026-year-to-date-in-review/) between January and September 2026. Alibaba's Qwen led with 29 models and NVIDIA added 17, including the Cosmos3 world models for robotics. Open models now reach a one-click cloud deployment within months of release.

Nader Dabit argues that a [new business is opening up in pen-testing small and mid-sized companies](https://x.com/dabit3/status/2112271077342695772) and then selling them contracts to stay safe. He says a shocking number of their vulnerabilities are open even to novice ethical hackers, and that the economics changed drastically in about six months.

And Simon Willison pointed to Jeremy Scheff's [obituary for programming](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/19/my-obituary-for-programming/), which mourns the job of getting paid to solve tricky little logic puzzles. Willison's take: if you love building working systems, coding agents keep on giving, but if the puzzles were the fun, the loss is very real.

That's the cycle. Go give your agent a check it can't fake, and see you in the morning.
